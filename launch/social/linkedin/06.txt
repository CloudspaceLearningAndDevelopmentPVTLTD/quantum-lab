Grover's algorithm finds one item in 256 after 12 queries. Run it a thirteenth time and it starts losing the answer.

Everyone teaches the first half: amplitude amplification, √N instead of N, 12 queries against the 128 a classical search expects. The half that gets left out is that each iteration is a rotation, and rotations do not stop when they arrive. Past the optimum the amplitude keeps turning — through the peak, back down, and at step 25 the marked item is almost exactly as hidden as it was before you started.

Which means Grover is not "run it until you find it". It is "run it exactly ⌊π/4 × √N⌋ times and then stop", and knowing N matters. If you do not know how many marked items there are, you do not know when to stop — which is a real problem with a real fix (quantum counting), and one of the many places where the speed-up is narrower than the headline.

The bars are exact amplitudes, not samples: a_k = sin((2k+1)θ) with sin θ = 1/√256. Watch the sea of 255 unmarked states dip below zero on the way back down.
